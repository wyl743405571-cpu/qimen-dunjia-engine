## The kind of Qi Men output you've probably already seen

If you've encountered Qi Men Dun Jia — through a practitioner or through AI tools on the market —
you've probably seen judgments like these:

- "This chart is highly inauspicious; nothing should be done."
- "The Death Door enters the palace; disaster may loom, so you should ask someone to resolve it."
- "An auspicious door shines upon you; wealth flows in abundantly."

These share a feature: they hand you an auspicious-or-inauspicious label and leave the rest to your
imagination. Worse is the other extreme: presenting Qi Men as fate — "you are destined to…" —
frightening, yet offering no actionable step.

We believe this is not a problem with Qi Men itself, but with how it is explained. Qi Men is a
symbolic system — the Nine Stars, Eight Doors, Eight Spirits, heavenly stems, and Nine Palaces — that
describes how energy flows at a particular time and in a particular space. It should help people *see
the situation clearly, evaluate timing, and decide on action* — not stamp a "good/bad" label.

## We treat it as a space-time decision system

Conclusion first: we use Qi Men as a space-time decision system, not a fortune-telling tool. This
affects every word of our output. We do three things other products rarely do.

### First: we show you the entire reasoning process

Most tools give only conclusions. We give the full chain: symbol → image → conclusion → action. For
example:

> Because the Duty Marshal falls into Kan-1 Palace (Water, governing sinking and concealment), it
> meets the Celestial Peng Star (risk, recklessness); when Water is abundant, Metal sinks and the Day
> Stem is suppressed, so energy is blocked — therefore current risk is relatively high. We suggest
> you hold off on investing and act only when the situation clears.

You can see which symbols led to the conclusion and through what reasoning. It is not a black box.

### Second: every judgment is sourced

After each key conclusion there is a marker stating what symbol it is based on, what reasoning led to
it, and which tradition it comes from. We follow three rules:

- Use only symbols that genuinely appear in *your* chart. If something is absent, we say "not shown
  in the chart."
- We never fabricate page numbers from ancient texts. Sources are cited only to title and chapter.
- Auspicious/inauspicious statements have boundaries. Health → see a doctor. Legal/financial →
  "for reference only, not professional advice."

Trust comes from verifiability.

### Third: we translate terminology into language you can act on

Traditional Qi Men terminology is for those already in the know. But we serve someone facing a
concrete decision — whether to invest now, whether a relationship can be repaired, how to lay out a
shop. So while preserving complete professional reasoning, we restate the reading in plain language:

| Traditional term | Our plain-language rewrite |
| --- | --- |
| 五行 Five Elements | Five modes of energy movement: Wood = sprouting, Fire = radiating, Earth = bearing, Metal = gathering, Water = pervading |
| 八门 Eight Doors | Eight states of action: Open = passage opening, Life = fertile ground, Harm = friction rising, Death = something ending |
| 格局 Pattern | A friction-coefficient description — not success or failure |
| 应期 Timing | *When* a tendency is most likely to manifest |

A traditional reading might say "the Harm Door approaches your focal reference, indicating harm." We
would say: "this chart has relatively high friction — it may suit a forceful breakthrough, or a
temporary retreat, depending on whether you can bear the cost. The choice is yours."

We only illuminate the situation; we don't make the decision for you. That is our bottom line, and
why we keep our distance from the word "fortune-telling."
